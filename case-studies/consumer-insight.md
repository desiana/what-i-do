# Consumer Insight to Commercial Direction — Nordic Household Products

**Work type:** applied customer research and thesis work  
**Setting:** Nordic household-products manufacturer  
**Scope:** plastic storage boxes in household and personal use  
**Status:** research and recommendations completed; adoption and commercial outcomes not claimed  
**Last reviewed:** 14 September 2026

## Overview

This work demonstrates how I enter an unfamiliar product category, structure fragmented customer evidence and translate it into decisions about products, positioning, messaging and the buying journey.

The research began by asking what customers were trying to achieve, what made storage products fail in real situations, what people trusted or distrusted and how they naturally described a good solution.

## Research questions

- What emotional drivers and motivations should communication address?
- Which problems recur most often?
- What language do customers naturally use?
- Which outcomes matter beyond individual features?
- What creates frustration, distrust or purchase hesitation?
- What does a good solution look like in different situations?

The intended output was **messaging hypotheses to test**, not unsupported certainty.

## Method

### Exploratory qualitative study

A related exploratory study analysed 70 customer quotations from public social media, forums and product-review environments across several markets.

I organised the evidence around psychographic segments, journey stages, motivations, aspirational identity, practical and emotional outcomes, urgency triggers, objections and repeated customer language.

### Expanded mixed-evidence research

The thesis research combined:

- **121 survey responses**;
- **76 public online discussions**;
- **402 product reviews**.

The study was primarily European in focus. Qualitative patterns were organised across 15 topics and examined alongside quantitative evidence so repeated experiences could be distinguished from isolated comments.

## What the evidence showed

Four connected patterns emerged:

1. Core product features shape reliability and usability.
2. Features become meaningful when they help the product work in a specific situation.
3. Storage boxes support protection, organisation and manageability rather than storage as an abstract goal.
4. Customers judge whether a box is worth buying and worth the money; when fit is incomplete, they adapt their behaviour or add their own solutions.

Customers often began with a storage problem, location, item or life situation rather than the manufacturer's internal product-series structure.

This changed the strategic question to:

> How can communication help a shopper recognise their mission, select an appropriate product and feel confident about the choice?

## Recommendations designed

### Consumer missions as a navigation layer

I proposed organising communication around practical missions such as location, stored contents, events, life situations and jobs-to-be-done:

**Storage problem → relevant mission → suitable product → concrete proof**

### Match message style to the decision

- **Benefit-led communication** for familiar, lower-risk situations.
- **Feature-led communication** when technical differences and failure risk matter.
- **Hybrid communication** that begins with the mission and follows with a reason to believe.

### Connect offline and online touchpoints

I designed complementary roles for point-of-sale communication, packaging, mission-selection pages, product-detail pages, reviews, size-and-space guidance, nearby availability, compatible-product guidance and post-purchase support.

### Reduce uncertainty

Recommendations connected recurring concerns to decision support around durability, closure, stackability, compatibility, visibility, retrieval, space fit, long-term reviews, guarantees and the translation from specification to customer benefit.

### Test before scaling

I proposed a bounded pilot using one consumer mission, one product, one retail context and one connected shelf, packaging and digital journey.

The pilot would test whether shoppers understood the intended use and whether selection confidence improved.
